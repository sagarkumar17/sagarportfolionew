# Morgan Stanley KIID Repository

**Morgan Stanley · Digital Vault**

*A secure digital vault for client–advisor document exchange.*

A shared repository gives clients and financial advisors a secure place to store, exchange and discuss important financial documents.

> Journey: Client ↔ advisor · permissions · document collaboration

## The brief

### Sensitive document exchange needs visible trust

**The problem.** Clients and financial advisors needed to store, share and discuss sensitive financial documents. Internal teams also needed a usable overview across repositories, channels, document types and activity.

**The solution.** The documented direction organizes around client–advisor relationships and document lifecycle, with visible permissions, two-way sharing and filterable operational oversight.

**The outcome.** The project delivered an end-to-end portal design for secure client–advisor document exchange, with shared repository oversight for global operations.

Clients and financial advisors exchange documents that can be deeply personal and highly sensitive. Email makes sharing easy to start but difficult to govern: permissions are hard to inspect, versions and discussion become scattered, and internal teams have little visibility across repositories. The product needed to serve advisors who work in it every day, clients who return occasionally for high-stakes tasks, and operations teams overseeing activity across a global environment.

## What the research revealed

### Five findings shaped the direction

#### 01 / Information architecture was the product

Multiple repositories, channels and permission layers made content organization the central usability problem. The relationship between people and documents needed to lead the structure.

#### 02 / Trust needed to be visible

Clients sharing wills and estate plans needed clear access, permission and activity cues in the interface rather than assurances hidden in settings.

#### 03 / One system served two different rhythms

Advisors were frequent power users; clients were occasional users with high-stakes needs. Each needed a distinct view over shared information.

#### 04 / Operations needed to manage scale

Internal teams needed to segment repositories by channel, document type and activity instead of searching through an undifferentiated data set.

## Research synthesis

### Build around the client–advisor relationship

A relationship-based repository lets client and advisor tasks share one secure model without collapsing their different needs.

## Design strategy

### The design response

- Organize around the relationship users recognize, not a file-system metaphor.
- Show who can see, edit or comment at the point of action.
- Give clients and advisors role-appropriate views of the same repository.
- Use filters and segmentation to make global oversight manageable.
- Make security and permission behavior understandable in the workflow.

The design used the client–advisor relationship as the organizing model, then connected it to the document lifecycle. Permission state became visible on each document; sharing supported both directions; and operations received filters across repository counts, channels, document types and activity. This made the product a secure collaboration space for people and an operable oversight console for the organization.

## Key decisions

### Use one shared repository with role-aware views

#### Two audience needs

- **What we learned:** Advisors are frequent power users; clients are occasional and high-stakes.
- **Design response:** Use role-aware views over one shared repository model.

#### Relationship-based IA

- **What we learned:** People think in terms of documents shared with an advisor.
- **Design response:** Organize by client–advisor relationship and lifecycle.

#### Sensitive sharing

- **What we learned:** Permission clarity is essential for wills and estate documents.
- **Design response:** Make access and permission state visible.

#### Operations at scale

- **What we learned:** Internal teams need to inspect many repositories.
- **Design response:** Support filtering and segmentation for analysis.

## What we delivered

### From research through release

The end-to-end portal design covered repository overview, document viewing and commentary, two-way sharing, permission management and an operations dashboard. I led a distributed team of five designers—four offshore and one onshore—with two product owners, using shared artifacts to coordinate the work. The project also included the information architecture, user flows and a design system for the portal.

## Reflection

### What I learned and what I would do next

Starting with the information architecture made later design decisions clearer, and stakeholder interviews aligned business and user needs early. I would seek direct client research access sooner and prototype permission changes interactively, since static screens under-communicated the complexity of access states.
